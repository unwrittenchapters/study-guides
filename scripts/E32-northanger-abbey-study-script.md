## The book in brief

[Card slides in: 1817 · Bath and Gloucestershire, late 1790s]

[upbeat, storytelling]

Catherine Morland is seventeen, a clergyman's daughter who loves thrilling Gothic novels. She goes to Bath with her neighbours, the Allens. There she meets the clever, teasing Henry Tilney and his gentle sister Eleanor. She also meets the boastful John Thorpe and his showy sister Isabella.

Henry's father, General Tilney, invites Catherine to stay at Northanger Abbey, the family home. Catherine hopes the old abbey hides dark secrets, like the castles in her books. Soon she starts to suspect the General of something terrible.

## The story, chapter by chapter

[storytelling, warm but lively]

Now for the story. Here's how it unfolds, chapter by chapter.

[Chapter card flips in: THE BLACK CABINET]

Chapter one. The Black Cabinet.

Catherine Morland had read The Mysteries of Udolpho, so she knew exactly what old abbeys were like.

They had secret passages, locked doors and mysterious papers hidden in old chests. So when General Tilney invited her to stay at Northanger Abbey, she could hardly breathe for excitement.

She rode the last part of the way in Henry's open carriage. Henry teased her the whole way. He told her a spooky story about her bedroom, with a dim lamp, an old chest and an ebony cabinet.

That night a storm howled round the abbey. And there in her room stood a tall black cabinet. Catherine's hands shook as she turned the key. Behind a small inner door, she found a roll of old paper. She trimmed her candle to see better, and it went out.

In the morning sunshine, she unrolled the paper. It was a washing bill. Shirts, stockings and cravats.

She went down to breakfast, red with shame. Henry was there alone.

[Chapter card flips in: THE WASHING BILL]

Chapter two. The Washing Bill.

Henry asked, with a twinkle, if the storm had kept her awake.

Catherine was a very bad liar. She opened her mouth to say "a little," and found she couldn't stop there.

"Yes," she said. "And it's partly your fault." Then she told him everything. The cabinet, the key, the candle going out, the long night under the covers. She ran upstairs, fetched the papers and laid them on the table beside the hyacinths.

Henry read the first one. "Shirts," he said gravely. "Stockings. A most dreadful secret."

Catherine laughed first. Then Henry laughed too, so hard he had to sit down.

"I'm sorry," he said at last. "I made up that cabinet to tease you. I never thought you'd find a real one."

"Neither did I," said Catherine.

Then the General came in, and they both had to look very serious about the teacups. After breakfast, Henry rode off to his parish at Woodston.

[Chapter card flips in: THE FIR WALK]

Chapter three. The Fir Walk.

That afternoon, Eleanor took Catherine along a narrow path through a grove of old fir trees.

"It was my mother's favourite walk," Eleanor said quietly. Her mother had died nine years before, when Eleanor was only thirteen.

A day earlier, Catherine might have imagined a terrible secret. But she remembered the washing bill, and she simply took Eleanor's hand. She asked what her mother had been like.

So Eleanor told her, and Catherine listened to every word.

Later that week, Eleanor showed her the room where her mother had lived. It was bright and pleasant, with a pretty bed and the sun pouring through two tall windows. There was nothing strange in it at all.

When Henry came home, Eleanor told him how kind Catherine had been. He looked at Catherine with something warmer than teasing.

[Chapter card flips in: THE LAST PAGE]

The Last Page.

A few days later, the General drove them all to Woodston to see Henry's house.

It was a cheerful home in a green village. A big Newfoundland puppy and some terriers came running out to meet them. Through the window, Catherine saw a little cottage among apple trees. She said it was the sweetest thing she'd ever seen.

That evening, back at the abbey, Henry read The Mysteries of Udolpho aloud by the fire, doing all the voices. Catherine laughed at the scary parts, and once, when a door creaked, she laughed at herself.

She'd come to Northanger hoping to find a mystery. Instead she'd found a true friend in Eleanor, and someone who laughed with her, never at her. And that was far better than any secret in a cabinet.

## The big ideas

[curious]

**Fiction and reality.** Catherine learns that real life isn't a Gothic novel.

**Growing up.** Catherine learns to trust her own judgement about people.

**True and false friends.** Isabella's sweet words hide selfish aims, while the Tilneys are sincere.

**Money and marriage.** The General and the Thorpes care more about fortune than feelings.

## The real ending

[clearly, storytelling]

So what actually happens in the book?

In the book, Catherine hides the washing bill and starts to suspect the General of harming his late wife. Henry catches her coming out of his mother's room and gently shows her how wrong she was. Soon after, the General learns she isn't rich and sends her home alone. But Henry follows her and proposes, and they marry within a year of first meeting.

[thoughtfully]

Why did Jane Austen choose it? Austen pokes fun at Gothic novels, and shows that real dangers, like greed and lies, are quieter than any ghost.

[Beat.]

## Meet the author

[upbeat]

Before we go, here's a little about who wrote it.

Jane Austen wrote the first version of this novel in her early twenties and called it *Susan*. A publisher bought it in 1803 for ten pounds but never printed it, so her brother Henry bought it back in 1816. Austen died in July 1817, aged forty-one, and the novel came out that December, together with *Persuasion*. Henry's short note inside named her as the author for the first time.

## The world of the book

[curious]

In Austen's day, Bath was a fashionable spa town. Families came to drink the waters, dance at the Assembly Rooms and meet in the Pump Room. Gothic novels like Ann Radcliffe's *The Mysteries of Udolpho* were hugely popular, full of castles, secret passages and hidden manuscripts. *Northanger Abbey* gently makes fun of them.
