## The book in brief

[Card slides in: 1847 · Lowood School, northern England, early 1800s]

[upbeat, storytelling]

Jane Eyre is an orphan raised by a cruel aunt. At ten she's sent to Lowood, a harsh charity school run by Mr Brocklehurst. There she meets the kind teacher Miss Temple and a patient older girl, Helen Burns, who becomes her best friend. Helen dies of illness during a fever outbreak.

After eight years at Lowood, Jane becomes a governess at Thornfield Hall and falls in love with its owner, Mr Rochester. On their wedding day she learns he's already married to a woman hidden in the attic, and she leaves.

## The story, chapter by chapter

[storytelling, warm but lively]

Now for the story. Here's how it unfolds, chapter by chapter.

[Chapter card flips in: LOWOOD]

Chapter one. Lowood.

Jane Eyre was ten years old when she came to Lowood.

She was an orphan. She'd been raised by an aunt who didn't love her, in a house where she was always in the way. So when her aunt sent her off to a charity school eighty miles away, Jane was almost glad. She thought anywhere must be better.

At first, Lowood wasn't better.

It was a long, grey stone building in a damp valley, with a garden inside high walls. Eighty girls lived there, all in brown dresses and plain little caps. The rooms were cold and the food was poor. On Jane's very first morning, the breakfast porridge was burnt. It was so bitter that none of the girls could eat it, and they all went hungry until dinner.

A stern, tall man called Mr Brocklehurst ran the school. He believed girls should be kept humble and hungry for the good of their souls. He wasn't there often, but when he was, everyone was frightened.

[Chapter card flips in: THE STOOL]

Chapter two. The Stool.

Helen Burns was the most patient person Jane had ever met.

She wasn't always good at school. She forgot things, her drawers were untidy, and during lessons her mind wandered off to faraway places. One of the teachers, Miss Scatcherd, was always scolding her and punishing her in front of the class. And Helen never cried or complained, or said a single unkind word.

Jane couldn't understand it. If anyone treated her like that, she said, she'd fight back.

Helen only shook her head and said something Jane remembered for the rest of her life.

She means life's too short to hold grudges, or to keep a list of everyone who's hurt you.

A few weeks later, Jane needed that lesson.

[Chapter card flips in: THE SEA AIR]

Chapter three. The Sea Air.

Spring came to Lowood very late that year, and all at once.

The snow melted, and the beck, the little river in the valley, ran loud and brown and full. Snowdrops and crocuses came up in the garden beds, and then primroses, and the woods around the school turned green. Jane had been cold all winter. Now she ran about the hills on Thursday afternoons and thought she'd never seen anything so beautiful.

But Helen didn't come with her. She was tired, and she was coughing more.

Tonight, Miss Temple didn't wait.

She'd been thinking ever since the evening of the seed-cake. She knew a cold, damp valley, poor food and a crowded dormitory were the worst things in the world for a girl with a cough. So she wrote a letter. She didn't write to Mr Brocklehurst, who would only have said no. She wrote to her own sister, who lived in a little cottage by the sea.

[Chapter card flips in: THE LAST PAGE]

The Last Page.

Jane and Helen grew up together at Lowood.

They were pupils for six more years, and good ones. Jane was clever at drawing and French. Helen was clever at everything, when she remembered to be. When they were old enough, they both stayed on as teachers. They worked side by side in the same long classroom where Jane had once stood on a stool and been called a liar.

Now, whenever a new girl arrived cold and frightened and alone, one of them always went to find her on her first afternoon. They'd sit with her and ask what she was reading.

Tonight, it was Helen who read the advertisement with her by candlelight in their little room, and Helen who told her to go.

Jane was frightened. But Helen reminded her of something Jane had once said herself, when she was small and angry and standing up for herself. Years later at Thornfield, Jane would say it again to Mr Rochester, in words readers still remember.

## The big ideas

[curious]

**Independence.** Jane insists on being treated as a free, equal person.

**Forgiveness.** Helen teaches Jane that life is too short for grudges.

**Cruelty and kindness.** Lowood shows both, Brocklehurst's coldness and Miss Temple's care.

**Friendship.** Helen shows what one true friend can mean to Jane.

[curious]

So what could have happened?

[Path card: A · The fever still comes]

Path A. Helen stays at Lowood and falls ill when the fever comes. It could happen: Lowood is cold and damp, and the fever spreads fast. But Helen would be far away by the sea.

[Path card: B · No money for it]

Path B. Mr Brocklehurst refuses to pay, and Helen has to stay. It could happen: Mr Brocklehurst cuts costs everywhere. But Miss Temple has gone around his rules before, like ordering bread and cheese for the girls.

[Path card: C · Helen gets well]

Path C. Helen recovers in the sea air, writes to Jane every Saturday, and comes back strong in September. This one fits the characters: Miss Temple already cares for Helen. Sea air was a common cure for weak lungs.

## The real ending

[clearly, storytelling]

So what actually happens in the book?

In the book, typhus sweeps through Lowood, and Helen dies of consumption in Jane's arms. Her faith in forgiveness stays with Jane for the rest of her life.

[thoughtfully]

Why did Charlotte Brontë choose it? Brontë based Helen on her own older sisters, Maria and Elizabeth, who died after falling ill at their boarding school.

[Beat.]

## Meet the author

[upbeat]

Before we go, here's a little about who wrote it.

Charlotte Brontë grew up in Haworth, a village on the Yorkshire moors, with her sisters Emily and Anne and her brother Branwell. As children they wrote tiny books about imaginary kingdoms. Charlotte and Emily were sent to a harsh school where two older sisters became ill and died. That school inspired Lowood.

## The world of the book

[curious]

In the early 1800s, charity schools took in poor girls cheaply, and conditions were often hard. Governess work was one of the few jobs open to an educated woman without money. Brontë published under the name Currer Bell, because women writers weren't always taken seriously.
