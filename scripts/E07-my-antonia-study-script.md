## The book in brief

[Card slides in: 1918 · Nebraska prairie, 1880s to 1910s]

[upbeat, storytelling]

Ten-year-old Jim Burden, an orphan from Virginia, comes to live with his grandparents in Nebraska. On the same train is the Shimerda family, newly arrived from Bohemia, including a girl named Ántonia.

Jim teaches Ántonia English. Her family struggles through hard winters, and her father dies. As they grow up, Ántonia works in the fields and later as a hired girl in the town of Black Hawk.

## The story, chapter by chapter

[storytelling, warm but lively]

Now for the story. Here's how it unfolds, chapter by chapter.

[Chapter card flips in: THE EDGE OF THE WORLD]

Chapter one. The Edge of the World.

Jim Burden was ten years old when he came to Nebraska.

His mother and father had both died that year, back home in Virginia. He was sent west on the train to live with his grandparents, who had a farm out on the prairie near a little town called Black Hawk. He travelled with a young farmhand named Jake, who was going west to work for them. The trip took days and days, and Jim read a book about Jesse James the whole way.

In the crowded car up front, on the same train, was a family who'd come all the way across the ocean from Bohemia. They spoke no English. There was a mother and father, a big son and a little sister. There was also a girl of about fourteen, with big warm eyes and brown cheeks, who looked at Jim very curiously. Her name was Ántonia.

[Chapter card flips in: THE PUMPKINS]

Chapter two. The Pumpkins.

Jim's grandparents' farm was a sturdy wooden house set down in the middle of all that grass. It had a garden behind it and a big cottonwood tree.

One afternoon soon after he arrived, his grandmother went out to dig potatoes in the garden, and Jim went with her. The garden was a long way from the house, at the end of a path through the tall grass. When they got there, she told him he could stay out and rest a while if he liked. Then she walked back to the house.

Jim sat down in the warm dirt in the middle of the garden and leaned back against a big yellow pumpkin.

[Chapter card flips in: THE SCHOOLHOUSE]

Chapter three. The Schoolhouse.

Tonight, when the time came for Jim to leave, he didn't go.

He was eighteen and had finished at the little high school in Black Hawk. Everyone said he was clever. They said he ought to go to the university in Lincoln and then on to the East, and make something of himself. His grandparents had saved the money, and the train ticket was bought.

On the last evening, Jim walked out alone, past the garden and the cottonwood tree, into the tall grass. He stood there as the sun went down.

The whole prairie was turning red and gold. The grass moved in long, slow waves all the way to the edge of the sky, and a meadowlark was singing somewhere. Jim thought about the city, with its stone streets and crowded rooms and noise. He thought about the long years of remembering this.

[Chapter card flips in: THE LAST PAGE]

The Last Page.

One evening in late summer, Jim was walking home across the prairie from the Cuzaks' farm. He'd eaten supper there with the whole noisy family.

The sun was going down, huge and red, right at the edge of the world. As he walked, he saw something on a rise in the distance that made him stop.

Someone had left a plough standing in a field, and the sun was sinking exactly behind it. The plough stood out black against the red, with its handles, blade and long beam all sharp and clear. In the strange flat light it looked enormous, like a giant tool written on the face of the sun.

Tonight, Jim stood in the grass and watched it until the sun was gone. Then he walked on home in the dusk, with the crickets starting up and the first stars coming out, the way he'd done ten thousand times before.

## The big ideas

[curious]

**Memory.** Most of the book is Jim remembering. A what-if asks what happens if he never has to.

**Immigrant life.** Cather shows families from many countries building new lives on hard land.

**Land and belonging.** The prairie shapes everyone who lives on it.

**Ordinary heroism.** Ántonia's courage is in daily work, not big adventures.

[Beat. Music sting.]

## The real ending

[clearly, storytelling]

So what actually happens in the book?

In the book, Jim leaves for university and becomes a lawyer in New York. Twenty years later he visits Ántonia, now a farmer's wife with a big, happy family.

[thoughtfully]

Why did Willa Cather choose it? Cather shows how memory works. Jim's best years live on in his mind, while Ántonia builds a full life on the land he left.

[Beat.]

## Meet the author

[upbeat]

Before we go, here's a little about who wrote it.

Willa Cather moved from Virginia to Nebraska when she was nine, just like Jim. She grew up near Red Cloud among immigrant families from Bohemia, Sweden and Germany. She became a journalist and editor in New York, and later won the Pulitzer Prize. Ántonia was inspired by a real friend, Annie Pavelka.

## The world of the book

[curious]

In the 1880s, the Homestead Act let families claim prairie land if they lived on it and farmed it. Many settlers built houses of sod because there were almost no trees. Winters were brutal, and many families arrived knowing no English at all.
