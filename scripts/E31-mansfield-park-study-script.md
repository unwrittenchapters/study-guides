## The book in brief

[Card slides in: 1814 · Northamptonshire and Portsmouth, early 1800s]

[upbeat, storytelling]

Fanny Price is ten when she's sent from her crowded home in Portsmouth to live with rich relatives at Mansfield Park. Her bossy aunt, Mrs Norris, never lets her forget she's the poor relation. Only her cousin Edmund treats her with real kindness.

Years later, while her uncle, Sir Thomas, is away at his estate in Antigua, the charming Crawfords arrive from London. The young people decide to put on a daring play called Lovers' Vows. Edmund falls for the witty Mary Crawford, while Fanny quietly watches and refuses to act.

## The story, chapter by chapter

[storytelling, warm but lively]

Now for the story. Here's how it unfolds, chapter by chapter.

[Chapter card flips in: THE EAST ROOM]

Chapter one. The East Room.

Fanny Price was ten years old when she came to live at Mansfield Park.

Her own family in Portsmouth was large, poor and noisy. Her cousins thought she was dull, and Aunt Norris kept telling her to be grateful. One day her cousin Edmund found her crying on the attic stairs. She missed her brother William. So Edmund found her paper and helped her write him a letter.

Years passed. Sir Thomas sailed to Antigua, and the lively Crawfords arrived from London. The young people decided to act a play called Lovers' Vows, and Fanny refused to take part. Then her cousin Tom said he'd invite Charles Maddox, a young man they barely knew, to play Anhalt opposite Mary Crawford.

The next morning, Edmund knocked on the door of the East room, Fanny's little sitting room. He said he must take the part himself.

"Can you think of anything else, Fanny?"

"No," she said slowly, "not immediately, but..."

In the book, he doesn't wait. This time, he did.

[Chapter card flips in: FANNY'S ANSWER]

Chapter two. Fanny's Answer.

Edmund sat down by the window and waited.

Fanny's heart was beating fast. She wasn't used to anyone waiting for her opinion. Her geraniums stood on the windowsill, and her books were piled on the table. There was no fire, because Aunt Norris said she didn't need one.

"You were against the play from the start," she said at last. "You said my uncle wouldn't like it. I'm sorry for Miss Crawford, but I'm sorrier to see you give in."

"Last night Miss Crawford told me she wasn't hopeful about the play. She said acting with a stranger would be horrid. If you asked her honestly, perhaps she'd rather stop too."

Edmund looked at her for a long moment. Then he smiled.

"Fanny," he said, "you're quite right. I think I knew it when I came in. I just didn't want to hear it."

He'd walk down to the Parsonage after breakfast, he said, and talk to Miss Crawford.

[Chapter card flips in: THE GREEN CURTAIN]

Chapter three. The Green Curtain.

Mary Crawford laughed when Edmund explained.

"Oh, thank goodness," she said. "I was dreading those rehearsals with Mr Maddox." She gave up the part of Amelia that very afternoon.

Without an Amelia or an Anhalt, the play began to fall apart. Tom asked his sister Julia to play Amelia, and Julia said certainly not. Within a week, even Tom was bored. He'd always lost interest in things quickly.

Mrs Norris was the most upset. She'd ordered yards of green cloth for the stage curtain. In the end she folded it up and carried it home, because she said it would be useful.

A few days later, a carriage came up the drive. Sir Thomas was home early from Antigua. He found his family sitting quietly in the drawing room. Edmund told him the whole story.

"Fanny was right from the beginning," he said.

[Chapter card flips in: THE LAST PAGE]

The Last Page.

Sir Thomas looked at his niece for a long time. He gave orders that a fire should be lit in the East room every day.

That winter, the little room was warm at last. Fanny sat by the fire with her books and her plants, and Edmund came up most mornings to talk. He told her about the sermons he was planning, and the village parish he'd soon look after. He asked her opinion, and he always let her finish.

Edmund was slow to understand his own heart. Austen's Edmund always was. But one bright spring morning, he looked up from his book and saw Fanny smiling by the window. And he wondered why he'd never noticed how much he looked forward to that smile.

## The big ideas

[curious]

**Conscience.** Fanny's quiet sense of right holds firm when everyone pushes her.

**Charm and principle.** The Crawfords are clever and fun, but they lack firm principles.

**Upbringing.** Sir Thomas taught his daughters fine manners, but not how to be good.

**Home and belonging.** The poor relation slowly becomes the heart of Mansfield.

## The real ending

[clearly, storytelling]

So what actually happens in the book?

In the book, Edmund agrees to play Anhalt opposite Mary. Sir Thomas comes home early in the middle of a rehearsal and burns every copy of the play he can find. Later, Maria Bertram marries the rich, dull Mr Rushworth, then runs off with Henry Crawford. Mary's careless reaction finally opens Edmund's eyes, and he marries Fanny.

[thoughtfully]

Why did Jane Austen choose it? Austen shows that quiet, steady principle matters more than charm, and that Fanny was right all along.

[Beat.]

## Meet the author

[upbeat]

Before we go, here's a little about who wrote it.

Jane Austen was born in 1775 in Steventon, Hampshire, where her father was the rector. She wrote *Mansfield Park* between 1811 and 1813 at her cottage in Chawton. It came out in 1814 without her name, and the first printing sold out within six months. Two of her brothers were officers in the Royal Navy, like Fanny's brother William.

## The world of the book

[curious]

Sir Thomas owns property in Antigua, a Caribbean island where sugar plantations were worked by enslaved people. Britain had banned the slave trade in 1807, but slavery itself went on in its colonies. When Fanny asks her uncle about the slave trade, the room falls silent.
