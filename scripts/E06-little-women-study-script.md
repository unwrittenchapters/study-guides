## The book in brief

[Card slides in: 1868 to 1869 · Concord, Massachusetts, and New York, 1860s]

[upbeat, storytelling]

The four March sisters, Meg, Jo, Beth and Amy, live with their mother, Marmee, while their father serves in the Civil War. They're poor, but their home is full of love, plays and writing. Next door lives Laurie, a lonely rich boy who becomes their best friend.

Jo wants to be a writer and hates the idea of giving up her freedom. As they grow up, Meg marries, Beth falls ill, and Jo goes to New York to work and write. There she meets Professor Bhaer.

## The story, chapter by chapter

[storytelling, warm but lively]

Now for the story. Here's how it unfolds, chapter by chapter.

[Chapter card flips in: THE WALK HOME]

Chapter one. The Walk Home.

Jo March had come home from New York in the summer, and Laurie had come home from college. The whole town knew what was going to happen, except maybe Jo.

She'd gone to New York the winter before, partly to work and partly to get away. She'd seen it coming, the way you see weather coming. She'd thought that if she went away for a while, the feeling might blow over. She taught children in a boarding house and wrote stories for the papers at night. She made friends with a kind, shabby German professor who lived down the hall. And she came home with a little money and a great many new ideas, and the feeling was still waiting for her exactly where she'd left it.

[Chapter card flips in: THE GARRET]

Chapter two. The Garret.

They were married the next spring in the March family's little parlour, with the apple trees in blossom outside and the whole household crowded in. Meg cried and Amy arranged the flowers. Marmee kissed Jo on the forehead and said nothing, which was how Jo knew she was moved. Old Mr Laurence, Laurie's grandfather, gave Jo away in place of her father, who led the service himself.

It was a small, plain wedding. Jo would have hated anything else.

Everyone expected them to live in the big Laurence house, but they didn't. Jo said it was too grand, and she'd always feel she ought to be dusting it. Instead they took a smaller house at the end of the same street. It had a garden and a stable, and a long, low attic under the roof that Jo claimed for her own on the very first day.

[Chapter card flips in: LIBERTY]

Chapter three. Liberty.

Jo had always been afraid of one thing. She'd said it out loud more than once, long before Laurie ever asked her anything.

Liberty means freedom, being free to live your own way, and she meant it. She'd watched women give up their names, their money, their time and their thoughts when they married. She'd watched clever girls become tired wives, with no room left in their days for anything of their own. At fifteen, with her hair cut short and her chin in the air, she'd decided it would never happen to her.

So in that first winter, when the snow came and the house grew quiet, she watched herself very carefully. It was the way you might watch a pot to see if it's going to boil over. She waited for the moment her liberty would start to go.

It didn't come.

[Chapter card flips in: THE LAST PAGE]

The Last Page.

Tonight, the professor came to visit them once, on his way to a new post in the West. He was as kind and shabby as ever. He brought Jo a book of German poems and sat by their fire all evening. He talked about Shakespeare with Laurie until midnight, and the next morning he went away with a basket of Marmee's apples. Jo was sorry to see him go. He'd been a good friend to her in New York, when she was lonely.

As she watched his train pull out, she thought about how many good lives a person might live. The one you choose isn't the only right one. It's just the one that's yours.

In this story too, Aunt March left Plumfield to Jo. And Jo and Laurie did exactly what anyone who knew them would have expected, and filled it.

## The big ideas

[curious]

**Liberty.** Jo fears that marriage will take her freedom. The book answers that fear in a surprising way.

**Work and art.** Jo writes for money and for love, and must decide which stories are true to her.

**Family.** The March home is poor but full of warmth.

**Choices.** The book suggests there are many good lives, and the right one is the one that's yours.

## The real ending

[clearly, storytelling]

So what actually happens in the book?

In the book, Jo says no. Laurie marries her sister Amy, and Jo later marries Professor Bhaer and opens a school with him.

[thoughtfully]

Why did Louisa May Alcott choose it? Alcott wanted Jo to stay single, but her publisher and readers pushed for a wedding. So she gave Jo an unexpected husband instead of the one everyone expected.

[Beat.]

## Meet the author

[upbeat]

Before we go, here's a little about who wrote it.

Louisa May Alcott grew up in Concord, Massachusetts, the second of four sisters, just like Jo. Her family was often poor, and she worked as a teacher, a seamstress and a nurse in the Civil War. A publisher asked her to write a book for girls, and she based it on her own family. It was such a hit that she wrote the second part within months.

## The world of the book

[curious]

The first part takes place during the American Civil War, while Mr March serves as an army chaplain. Women had few choices of work then. Writing for magazines was one of the few ways a woman could earn her own money, which is why Jo's stories matter so much.
