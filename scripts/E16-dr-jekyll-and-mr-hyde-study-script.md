## The book in brief

[Card slides in: 1886 · London, in the late 1800s]

[upbeat, storytelling]

Mr Utterson, a London lawyer, hears a strange story from his cousin, Richard Enfield. Late one night, a man called Edward Hyde knocked down a young girl and walked right over her. To pay her family, he used a cheque signed by the respected Dr Henry Jekyll.

Utterson is worried, because Jekyll's will leaves everything to Hyde. Nearly a year later, Hyde kills an old gentleman, Sir Danvers Carew, in the street and disappears. Then Jekyll shuts himself away, and Utterson sets out to learn why.

## The story, chapter by chapter

[storytelling, warm but lively]

Now for the story. Here's how it unfolds, chapter by chapter.

[Chapter card flips in: THE DOOR]

Chapter one. The Door.

Mr Utterson wasn't a man who smiled much.

He was a serious London lawyer, but his friends knew he was kind underneath. Every Sunday he went for a long walk with his cousin, Richard Enfield. One day they passed an old door in a back street. It had no bell and no knocker, and its paint was cracked and peeling.

Enfield pointed at it with his cane. Late one night, he said, he'd seen a man trample right over a little girl. The man, called Hyde, went through that door and came back with a cheque for the girl's family. It was signed by a very respected name.

Utterson knew whose name it was. His old friend Dr Jekyll had written a will leaving everything to this Mr Hyde.

So Utterson watched the door, night after night, until he met Hyde face to face. Two weeks later, after one of Jekyll's dinners, he stayed behind to ask his friend about him.

[Chapter card flips in: THE TRUTH]

Chapter two. The Truth.

The other guests had gone home, and the fire was burning low.

Jekyll was a large, handsome man of about fifty. When Utterson mentioned the will, his face went pale. For a long time he said nothing at all. Then he stood up and said, "Come with me."

He led Utterson across the dark yard to his laboratory, and up the stairs to a small room he called his cabinet. He unlocked a drawer and took out a little paper of white powder.

Then he told Utterson everything. Years ago, he'd found a way to change his body with a drink. When he drank it, he became Edward Hyde. Hyde could do whatever he liked, and nobody would ever blame the good Dr Jekyll.

"At first it felt like freedom," Jekyll said quietly. "Now I'm afraid of him."

Utterson didn't shout, and he didn't lecture. He put his hand on his friend's shoulder.

"Then let me help you," he said.

[Chapter card flips in: THE KEY]

Chapter three. The Key.

They made a plan that very night.

First, Jekyll gave Utterson the key to the old back door, the one with no bell or knocker. Then he took every paper of powder from the drawer and dropped them into the fire. The two men watched until there was nothing left but ash.

Next, Utterson wrote to Dr Lanyon. The two doctors had quarrelled years before over Jekyll's strange science. But Lanyon was a warm, cheerful man, and when he heard his old friend was in trouble, he came at once.

The first weeks were hard. Some nights Jekyll couldn't sleep, and he paced his cabinet. Part of him missed being Hyde. On those nights Poole would knock softly and bring him tea. In the morning, Utterson always called on his way to work.

Slowly, the bad nights grew fewer. Jekyll went back to helping the poor, and to church, and to his friends. Every day he felt a little more like himself.

[Chapter card flips in: THE LAST PAGE]

The Last Page.

Nearly a year later, on a foggy autumn evening, Utterson and Enfield took their Sunday walk again.

They turned into the little back street, and Enfield stopped in surprise. The old door was gone. Builders had bricked up the doorway, and someone had set a pot of red flowers on the step.

As they walked on, an old gentleman with white hair strolled safely past them in the fog. He lifted his hat, and they lifted theirs.

That evening, the two cousins went on to Jekyll's house for dinner. Lanyon was already there, laughing loudly at his own jokes. Poole lit the lamps, and the three old friends sat round the table just as they had when they were young.

At the end of the meal, Jekyll raised his glass to Utterson. He didn't say why, and he didn't need to. Utterson understood.

## The big ideas

[curious]

**Two sides.** Stevenson suggests every person has a good side and a darker side.

**Reputation.** Victorian gentlemen hide their secrets to protect their good names.

**Science and its limits.** Jekyll's experiment goes further than anyone should go.

**Silence.** Utterson and his friends avoid hard questions, so the secret keeps growing.

[Beat. Music sting.]

## Our what-if

[curious]

In an alternative scenario, what if Jekyll tells Utterson the whole truth, that night after dinner?

[thoughtful]

So what could have happened?

[Path card: A · Utterson calls the police]

Path A. Utterson goes straight to the police. It could happen: Utterson is a careful lawyer who respects the law. But Hyde hasn't killed anyone yet, and Utterson wants to save his friend, not ruin him.

[Path card: B · Utterson walks out]

Path B. Utterson decides it's madness and walks out. It could happen: The story sounds impossible, and Utterson is a practical man. But he's seen Hyde's strange face for himself, and he's been worried for weeks.

[Path card: C · Friends keep watch]

Path C. Utterson takes the key, the powders are burned, and Jekyll's old friends keep watch. This one fits the characters: Stevenson tells us Utterson would always rather help than scold. And Lanyon is still a friend at heart.

## The real ending

[clearly, storytelling]

So what actually happens in the book?

In the book, Jekyll keeps his secret. Lanyon sees Hyde turn back into Jekyll and dies of the shock, and Jekyll starts becoming Hyde without the drink. At last, Utterson and Poole break into the cabinet and find Hyde dead by his own hand, with Jekyll's written confession waiting for Utterson.

[thoughtfully]

Why did Robert Louis Stevenson choose it? Stevenson shows that hiding the darker side of yourself doesn't get rid of it. It only makes it stronger.

[Beat.]

## Meet the author

[upbeat]

Before we go, here's a little about who wrote it.

Robert Louis Stevenson was born in Edinburgh in 1850 and was often ill as a child. He was already famous for *Treasure Island* when he wrote this story in 1885, in Bournemouth, on England's south coast. He said part of the idea came to him in a dream. He later moved to Samoa, in the Pacific, where he died in 1894.

## The world of the book

[curious]

Victorian London was a city of gaslight and fog, where grand houses stood close to poor, crowded streets. Respectable people were expected to hide anything shameful. Stevenson grew up hearing about Deacon Brodie, a respected Edinburgh craftsman who was secretly a burglar. He even co-wrote a play about him.
