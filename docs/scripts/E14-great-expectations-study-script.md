## The book in brief

[Card slides in: 1861 · The Kent marshes and London, early 1800s]

[upbeat, storytelling]

Pip, an orphan, is raised by his harsh sister and her gentle husband, Joe Gargery, the village blacksmith. On the marshes one Christmas Eve he's forced to help an escaped convict.

He's sent to play at Satis House, where Miss Havisham sits in her old wedding dress with all the clocks stopped. Her proud ward, Estella, makes Pip ashamed of his coarse hands and his home.

A lawyer, Mr Jaggers, announces that Pip has "great expectations" from a secret helper. Pip becomes a gentleman in London and neglects Joe. He learns that his helper is the convict, Magwitch, not Miss Havisham. He loses his fortune, and Joe nurses him back to health.

## The story, chapter by chapter

[storytelling, warm but lively]

Now for the story. Our sleep story for this book is called "Pip Stays at the Forge." Here's how it unfolds, chapter by chapter.

[Chapter card flips in: THE FORGE]

Chapter one. The Forge.

Pip was an orphan who'd never known his mother or his father. His much older sister, Mrs Joe Gargery, brought him up "by hand," as people said in those days.

Mrs Joe wasn't a gentle person. She was tall and bony and wore a stiff apron covered in pins, and she was almost always cross. But she was married to the gentlest man in Kent.

Joe Gargery was the village blacksmith. He was a big, fair, mild man with curly flaxen hair, very blue eyes, and arms as strong as iron. He couldn't read or write very well, and he wasn't clever. But he was good all the way through, like a well-made horseshoe, and he loved Pip as if Pip were his own son.

[Chapter card flips in: SATIS HOUSE]

Chapter two. Satis House.

Then one day, Pip was sent to play at Satis House.

It belonged to a rich, strange old lady called Miss Havisham, who lived in the nearby town. Many years before, on her wedding day, the man she was going to marry hadn't come. From that moment, she stopped every clock in the house at twenty minutes to nine. She never took off her wedding dress, and she never let the sunlight in.

Pip was led by candlelight through dark corridors into a room where the curtains hadn't been opened in years. There sat Miss Havisham, all in faded white, with one shoe on and her wedding veil still on her grey hair. On a long table, under a heap of dust and cobwebs, stood what was left of her wedding cake.

With her was a girl about Pip's age named Estella. She was very pretty and very proud, and Miss Havisham had taught her to break hearts.

[Chapter card flips in: THE LAWYER]

Chapter three. The Lawyer.

Pip had been Joe's apprentice for four years when the stranger came.

It was a Saturday night at the village inn, the Three Jolly Bargemen. Pip and Joe were sitting by the fire with their neighbours when a tall, heavy, dark man walked in. He had a large head and bushy black eyebrows, and he smelled strongly of scented soap. He was a London lawyer named Mr Jaggers.

He asked to speak to Joe and Pip in private. So they walked home with him to the forge cottage and sat down in the best parlour, and Mr Jaggers told them his news.

A person whose name he couldn't tell them, he said, wished to make Pip a gentleman. Pip would leave the forge at once and go to London. He'd be given money, an education and fine clothes. He was, said Mr Jaggers, a young man of great expectations.

Pip thought his heart would stop.

[Chapter card flips in: THE LAST PAGE]

The Last Page.

It's a strange thing, but after that Pip was happier than he'd ever been.

He went back to the forge and worked beside Joe, and the work felt different now. He wasn't just Joe's apprentice anymore, because he was choosing to be there. When his apprenticeship was over, Joe had a new sign painted over the door in big white letters, and Pip spelled them out for him. They said GARGERY & PIP, BLACKSMITHS.

Joe stood and looked at it for most of an afternoon.

Earlier in the book, Joe pays an awkward visit to Pip's fine London rooms. As he leaves, he says something wise.

He means life is full of goodbyes, joined together the way a blacksmith joins pieces of iron. Joe talks like a blacksmith, because that's what he is.

Tonight, there was no parting.

## The big ideas

[curious]

**Shame and home.** Pip learns to be ashamed of the people who love him.

**Social class.** Dickens asks what really makes a "gentleman."

**Loyalty.** Joe never stops loving Pip.

**Choosing your life.** Our version imagines Pip choosing the forge freely.

## Our what-if

[excited]

So what's the what-if? Here's the turning point we changed.

In our story, Pip sits alone on the forge step the night after Jaggers's news and looks at his own rough hands. He isn't ashamed of them anymore.

Everything after that moment is new, but the characters still act the way their authors wrote them.

[Beat.]

## Meet the author

[upbeat]

Before we go, here's a little about who wrote it.

Charles Dickens wrote *Great Expectations* in 1860 and 1861, later in his life. It came out in weekly parts in his own magazine, *All the Year Round*. He changed the ending after a friend said it was too sad. Like Pip, Dickens had once felt ashamed of his family's poverty.

## The world of the book

[curious]

Victorian England was strongly divided by class. Gentlemen didn't work with their hands, and a blacksmith's boy had little chance of rising. Convicts could be sent to Australia for life, and that's where Magwitch made his secret fortune.
