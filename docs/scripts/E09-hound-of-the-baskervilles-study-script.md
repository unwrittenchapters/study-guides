## The book in brief

[Card slides in: 1902 · London and Dartmoor, Devon]

[upbeat, storytelling]

Dr Mortimer brings Holmes and Watson an old legend. A wicked Baskerville was once killed by a hellish hound, and the family has been cursed ever since. Sir Charles Baskerville has just died of fright, and giant paw prints were found near his body.

Watson goes to Dartmoor with the new heir, Sir Henry, while Holmes secretly watches from a stone hut. They meet the naturalist Stapleton and his sister, and the dangerous Grimpen Mire.

Holmes discovers that Stapleton is a hidden Baskerville heir who painted a huge dog with phosphorus to look like the legendary hound. On a foggy night the dog attacks Sir Henry, Holmes shoots it, and Stapleton vanishes into the mire.

## The story, chapter by chapter

[storytelling, warm but lively]

Now for the story. Here's how our version unfolds, chapter by chapter.

[Chapter card flips in: THE WALKING STICK]

Chapter one. The Walking Stick.

Like so many of Holmes's cases, it began with a visitor who forgot something.

One morning, Holmes and Watson came down to breakfast and found a walking stick. A caller they'd missed had left it behind the night before. It was a fine, thick piece of wood with a silver band below the handle. Holmes asked Watson what he could tell about its owner. Watson tried his best and guessed the owner was an elderly country doctor who walked a great deal.

Holmes was delighted, and then he gently told Watson he was wrong about nearly everything. The owner, he said, was a young man, friendly and absent-minded, who owned a dog. From the tooth marks on the stick, he added, it was a middle-sized dog.

Just then the doorbell rang, and the owner walked in. He was a tall, thin young doctor called Mortimer, with a spaniel at his heels.

[Chapter card flips in: THE MOOR]

Chapter two. The Moor.

The train carried them out of London through the green fields of the south, past red earth and little stone churches. Then the land began to rise and grow wilder, and it turned from green to brown.

Watson stood at the carriage window and watched it change. Beyond the farms he could see the moor itself, long, low hills the colour of heather. On their tops sat strange, jagged piles of grey granite that looked like the ruins of castles. The people there call them tors. A soft grey mist drifted over everything.

[Chapter card flips in: THE OLD MINE]

Chapter three. The Old Mine.

The footprints led a long way across the moor.

They went down into a hollow and up the other side. Then they ran along the edge of a great flat stretch that looked, in the dusk, like a smooth green lawn. Holmes knew better. This was the Grimpen Mire, a deep, soft bog where one careless step could swallow a pony. The bright green patches were the most dangerous of all. But the footprints went carefully round it, along a winding path of firmer ground, and Holmes followed them step by step.

On the far side, in a hillside, was the opening of an old tin mine. Men had dug there long ago and left, and the moor had grown over the stones. From inside came a very faint sound.

It wasn't a howl. It was a long, soft, unhappy whine, the sound a dog makes when it's been left alone too long.

Holmes lit his lantern.

[Chapter card flips in: THE LAST PAGE]

The Last Page.

Holmes came back to Baskerville Hall at sunrise, walking slowly across the moor as the mist lifted around him. Beside him, on a length of rope, walked the hound.

Watson had been up all night waiting by the window. When he saw them coming up the avenue, he nearly dropped his coffee.

Tonight it ended more quietly. Holmes told the police what he'd worked out, and they called on the butterfly collector the next morning. The old legend of the Baskerville hound was over for good.

And the hound itself stayed at Baskerville Hall.

## The big ideas

[curious]

**Science against superstition.** Holmes proves there's a natural explanation behind the legend.

**Noticing.** Holmes sees what others overlook.

**Place.** Dartmoor's mist, mires and tors make the story feel dangerous.

**Kindness.** Our version shows the "monster" was a victim too.

## Our what-if

[excited]

So what's the what-if? Here's the turning point we changed.

In our story, Holmes goes to the moor himself and walks out alone at dusk. He follows the wandering paw prints around the mire to an old tin mine, where the dog lies chained and starving.

Everything after that moment is new, but the characters still act the way their authors wrote them.

[Beat.]

## Meet the author

[upbeat]

Before we go, here's a little about who wrote it.

Arthur Conan Doyle had stopped writing Holmes stories in 1893, because he wanted to write historical novels instead. On a golfing holiday, a friend told him a Dartmoor legend about a ghostly hound. Conan Doyle realised the story needed a detective, and brought Holmes back, setting it before the Reichenbach Falls.

## The world of the book

[curious]

Dartmoor is a real moor in Devon, with granite tors, stone circles, old tin mines and dangerous bogs. In 1901 it was remote and wild, and a prison stood in the middle of it, which plays a part in the novel. Local legends of black dogs were common across England.
