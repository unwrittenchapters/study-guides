## The book in brief

[Card slides in: 1815 · Highbury, a village in Surrey, England]

[upbeat, storytelling]

Emma Woodhouse is clever, rich and sure she's a brilliant matchmaker. After her governess marries Mr Weston, Emma takes up a sweet, shy friend, Harriet Smith.

Harriet is loved by Robert Martin, a decent young farmer. Emma talks her out of his proposal, hoping she'll marry the vicar, Mr Elton. But Elton wants Emma, and Emma's schemes keep going wrong.

Her old friend Mr Knightley is the only person who tells her when she's wrong. After she's cruel to poor Miss Bates, his scolding helps her grow up. Harriet marries Robert Martin after all, and Emma realises she loves Mr Knightley.

## The story, chapter by chapter

[storytelling, warm but lively]

Now for the story. Here's how our version unfolds, chapter by chapter.

[Chapter card flips in: THE MATCHMAKER]

Chapter one. The Matchmaker.

Austen begins her book with one of the most famous sentences she ever wrote.

That means Emma was pretty, smart and well off, and almost nothing had ever gone wrong for her.

She lived with her father at a fine old house called Hartfield. Mr Woodhouse was a gentle, nervous man who worried about everything. He worried about draughts and damp and late dinners, and whether anyone was eating too much cake. His favourite food was a thin, warm porridge called gruel. He couldn't understand why everyone else didn't love it as much as he did.

For sixteen years, Emma had been looked after by her governess, Miss Taylor, who was more like an older sister. But Miss Taylor had just married a pleasant neighbour called Mr Weston and moved half a mile away. Emma was very proud of that, because she believed she'd made the match herself.

"Poor Miss Taylor," sighed Mr Woodhouse, who didn't like anyone getting married, ever.

[Chapter card flips in: THE LETTER]

Chapter two. The Letter.

One morning, Harriet came hurrying up the lane to Hartfield with her cheeks pink and a letter in her hand.

It was from Robert Martin, and it was a proposal of marriage.

She gave it to Emma to read and stood twisting her gloves while she waited. "What shall I do?" she said. "Dear Miss Woodhouse, what would you advise me to do?"

She reads the letter and is surprised to find it's a very good one, clear and honest and full of real feeling. But she's made up her mind about Mr Elton. So she tells Harriet very sweetly that of course she must decide for herself. Then she makes it quite plain that saying yes would end their friendship. Harriet would do anything for Emma, so she writes back and says no.

Tonight, Emma held the letter and began to read it, and then she stopped.

She'd remembered something.

[Chapter card flips in: ABBEY-MILL FARM]

Chapter three. Abbey-Mill Farm.

Harriet wanted to give her answer in person, but she was too shy to go alone. So Emma drove her in the little carriage along the lanes between the hedges, past the fields of Donwell, as a soft summer rain began to fall.

Abbey-Mill Farm sat in a hollow by the river, with orchards around it and a wide green meadow running down to the water. It wasn't grand. But the garden was neat and the windows shone, and smoke rose from the chimney. The whole place looked well loved.

Emma had never been there before, and she was a little ashamed of that.

Mrs Martin opened the door, and her two daughters came running out behind her. There was a great fuss of hugging and bonnets and "come in out of the wet." Emma was given the best chair in the parlour, by a warm fire. On the table sat a blue china bowl with a heap of walnuts in it.

[Chapter card flips in: THE LAST PAGE]

The Last Page.

Mr Knightley heard the news that same evening, because news always travels fast in Highbury.

He came over to Hartfield after dinner, as he always did. He sat with Mr Woodhouse and agreed the weather had been very damp, and he said no, thank you, to the gruel. When Mr Woodhouse had dozed off by the fire, he asked Emma if she'd walk with him in the garden.

The rain had stopped, and everything smelled of wet leaves. They walked up and down the shrubbery path for a long time without saying anything at all.

"I hear you went to Abbey-Mill," he said at last.

"They were very fine cows," said Emma.

Mr Knightley laughed. Then he stopped walking and looked at her, and he spoke in a different voice. "That was well done, Emma. That was very well done."

## The big ideas

[curious]

**Listening.** Emma's big lesson is to listen instead of decide for people.

**Class.** Emma looks down on farmers. The book shows that goodness matters more than rank.

**Growing up.** Emma is nearly twenty-one but still has a lot to learn.

**Honest friendship.** Mr Knightley shows that a true friend tells you the truth.

## Our what-if

[excited]

So what's the what-if? Here's the turning point we changed.

In our story, Emma remembers Mr Knightley's words just as Harriet hands her the letter. For the first time, she asks Harriet what *she* wants.

Everything after that moment is new, but the characters still act the way their authors wrote them.

[Beat.]

## Meet the author

[upbeat]

Before we go, here's a little about who wrote it.

Jane Austen wrote *Emma* while living in the cottage at Chawton, in Hampshire. It was the last novel published in her lifetime. The Prince Regent was a fan, and his librarian suggested she dedicate the book to him, which she did, politely. She died in 1817, aged forty-one.

## The world of the book

[curious]

Highbury is a small village where everyone knows everyone's business. Rank mattered a great deal. A gentleman's daughter like Emma wasn't expected to mix with farmers, and that's why she looks down on Robert Martin.
