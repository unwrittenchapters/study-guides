## The book in brief

[Card slides in: 1843 · London, Christmas Eve and Christmas Day]

[upbeat, storytelling]

Ebenezer Scrooge is a mean, rich moneylender. On Christmas Eve he refuses to give to charity and barely lets his clerk, Bob Cratchit, have the day off.

That night the ghost of his old partner, Jacob Marley, warns him to change. Three spirits then visit. The Ghost of Christmas Past shows him his lonely childhood. The Ghost of Christmas Present shows him the poor but happy Cratchit family and their sick son, Tiny Tim. The Ghost of Christmas Yet to Come shows him his own lonely death.

## The story, chapter by chapter

[storytelling, warm but lively]

Now for the story. Here's how it unfolds, chapter by chapter.

[Chapter card flips in: ONE COAL]

Chapter one. One Coal.

It was cold, bleak, biting weather that Christmas Eve, and foggy too.

The city clocks had only just gone three, but it was quite dark already. It hadn't been light all day. Candles flared in the office windows along the street, like ruddy smears on the thick brown air. The fog came pouring in at every chink and keyhole. Outside it was so thick that the houses across the street looked like ghosts.

In the counting-house of Scrooge and Marley, which was their office, the door was kept open so Scrooge could keep his eye on his clerk.

[Chapter card flips in: THE SCARF]

Chapter two. The Scarf.

Scrooge had got as far as the end of the street before he realised he'd left his scarf.

He didn't much care about the scarf. But it was a good, thick woollen one, and he'd paid for it. Scrooge didn't like leaving anything he'd paid for lying about where it might be stolen. So he turned back through the fog, muttering, and let himself in with his own key.

He was very surprised to see a light still burning in the clerk's tank.

He was even more surprised to find Bob Cratchit still sitting on his high stool, with the ledger open in front of him, blowing on his fingers.

Bob jumped so hard he nearly fell off the stool.

Stammering a little, he explained. He'd wanted the books finished before Christmas, so nothing would be wrong when Mr Scrooge came in on the twenty-sixth. He hadn't meant to be any trouble. And he hadn't used any extra candle, he said. It was the same candle.

[Chapter card flips in: HALFWAY THERE]

Chapter three. Halfway There.

Bob Cratchit went home late that night through the fog. The office was locked behind him and the ledger was finished, and for the first time all day, his hands were warm.

At the top of Cornhill, where the street sloped down, a lane of boys had made a slide on the ice. They were taking turns to run and skid down it, shrieking. Bob stopped and watched them for a moment. It was Christmas Eve, his hands were warm, and something had happened tonight that he couldn't quite believe. So he took a little run and went down the slide at the end of the line of boys. Then he went round and did it again, twenty times, in honour of its being Christmas Eve.

That part's in the book. Tonight he just did it a little later, and laughed a little harder.

[Chapter card flips in: THE LAST PAGE]

The Last Page.

Scrooge woke on Christmas morning in his own bed, with the sun shining and the bells ringing. He found that the spirits had done it all in one night.

He was so happy he laughed and cried and danced about his room, and he put his stockings on inside out. He flung open the window, and the air came in clear, bright and cold. He called down to a boy in the street and asked him what day it was. The boy said, "Why, Christmas Day." So Scrooge sent him running to the poultry shop for the prize turkey, the one as big as the boy. It was to go to Bob Cratchit's house in Camden Town, with no name on it.

All that's in the book, and it's one of the happiest pages ever written.

On the morning after Christmas, Bob Cratchit was late for work.

## The big ideas

[curious]

**Change.** Dickens believed anyone could change, even Scrooge.

**Poverty.** The Cratchits show how hard life was for working families in 1840s London.

**Small kindnesses.** Dickens shows that big changes can start with small acts.

**Generosity.** Scrooge's change is shown in what he gives.

[Beat. Music sting.]

## Our what-if

[curious]

In an alternative scenario, what if Bob stays late to finish the books, and Scrooge comes back for his scarf?

[thoughtful]

So what could have happened?

[Path card: A · Scrooge scolds him]

Path A. Scrooge scolds him for wasting coal and storms out. It could happen: Scrooge hates waste, especially coal. But Bob is staying late to finish Scrooge's own work.

[Path card: B · Scrooge ignores him]

Path B. Scrooge takes his scarf without a word and walks out. It could happen: Scrooge avoids people whenever he can. But he has to walk right past Bob's desk.

[Path card: C · One more coal]

Path C. Without a kind word, Scrooge adds one coal to Bob's fire and hears about Tiny Tim for the first time. This one fits the characters: The ghosts change Scrooge by showing him real lives. Bob talks about Tim with love, not complaint.

## The real ending

[clearly, storytelling]

So what actually happens in the book?

In the book, Scrooge grudgingly gives Bob Christmas Day off. That night three spirits visit him. The next morning he's a changed man. He sends the Cratchits a prize turkey, raises Bob's salary, and becomes a second father to Tiny Tim, who does not die.

[thoughtfully]

Why did Charles Dickens choose it? Dickens wanted readers to see those who are impoverished as real people, and to believe that anyone can change.

[Beat.]

## Meet the author

[upbeat]

Before we go, here's a little about who wrote it.

Charles Dickens knew poverty as a child. When he was twelve, his father was sent to a debtors' prison, and Charles worked in a factory pasting labels on pots of boot polish. He never forgot it. He wrote *A Christmas Carol* in about six weeks in 1843, and it sold out by Christmas Eve.

## The world of the book

[curious]

In the 1840s, many children in London worked long hours and went hungry. Dickens had just read a report on child labour that shocked him. He decided a story would reach more hearts than a political pamphlet. The book also helped bring back old Christmas customs, like family dinners and giving to the poor.
