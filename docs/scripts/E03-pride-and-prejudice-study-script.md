## The book in brief

[Card slides in: 1813 · Hertfordshire, England, about 1811]

[upbeat, storytelling]

Mrs Bennet has five daughters and no sons, so the family home will go to a cousin when Mr Bennet dies. She badly wants her girls to marry well. When a rich young man, Mr Bingley, rents Netherfield Hall nearby, she gets very excited.

Bingley falls for the eldest daughter, Jane. His proud friend Mr Darcy insults the second daughter, Elizabeth, at a ball, and she decides she dislikes him. A charming officer, Mr Wickham, tells her a false story about Darcy, and she believes it.

Darcy proposes, and Elizabeth turns him down angrily. He writes her a letter explaining the truth about Wickham. Slowly each of them sees their own pride and prejudice, and when Darcy quietly saves her family from scandal, she realises she loves him.

## The story, chapter by chapter

[storytelling, warm but lively]

Now for the story. Our sleep story for this book is called "A Longer Stay at Netherfield." Here's how it unfolds, chapter by chapter.

[Chapter card flips in: THREE MILES OF MUD]

Chapter one. Three Miles of Mud.

Like so many things in that family, it began with one of Mrs Bennet's schemes.

Jane, the eldest of the five Bennet sisters, had been invited to dine at Netherfield with Mr Bingley's sisters. Mrs Bennet wanted Jane to marry Mr Bingley very much, so she wouldn't let her take the carriage. Jane must go on horseback, she said, because it looked like rain. And if it rained, Jane would have to stay the night.

It did rain, all evening. Jane had ridden there in it and caught a bad cold. The next morning a note came from Netherfield to say she was too unwell to come home.

Her sister Elizabeth couldn't bear to sit at home while Jane was ill. There was no carriage to be had, and Elizabeth couldn't ride. So she did what she always did when something mattered, and she walked.

[Chapter card flips in: THE LIBRARY]

Chapter two. The Library.

Netherfield had a library, though not a very good one.

Mr Bingley had only lately taken the house, and he wasn't much of a reader. He cheerfully admitted he had more books than he ever looked into. The shelves were half empty, and most of the books there had come with the house. There were old sermons, old histories, and a few books of poems nobody had ever opened. But it was a quiet room with a good fire. On the fourth wet afternoon, Elizabeth found her way into it, looking for something to read to Jane.

Mr Darcy was already there.

[Chapter card flips in: THE LETTER]

Chapter three. The Letter.

After that afternoon, the evenings at Netherfield were a little different.

In most ways they were the same. Mr Bingley still talked cheerfully about everything. His sister Mrs Hurst still played cards with her husband, who still fell asleep on the sofa after dinner. And Caroline Bingley, who wanted to marry Mr Darcy herself, still sat as near him as she could and praised everything he did.

There's a famous evening like this in the book. Mr Darcy is writing a letter to his young sister Georgiana, and Miss Bingley watches every line. She praises how even his writing is and how long his letters are. She asks him to tell his sister she longs to see her, until he can hardly write at all. Elizabeth sits with her needlework, quietly and wickedly amused.

[Chapter card flips in: THE LAST PAGE]

The Last Page.

The rain stopped on a Sunday.

Elizabeth woke to an odd silence and lay wondering about it for a while. Then she realised it was the missing sound of rain on the roof. She went to the window. The garden was washed and shining, the sky was a clean pale blue, and there were birds everywhere.

Jane was well enough to travel, so the carriage was ordered. By the middle of the morning the two sisters were standing in the hall in their bonnets, saying goodbye.

Miss Bingley was very polite, and very glad to see them go. Mr Bingley was sorry and said so many times. As usual, Mr Darcy said almost nothing. But when he handed Elizabeth into the carriage, he held her hand a moment longer than he strictly needed to. And he said quietly, "I hope your sister continues well."

## The big ideas

[curious]

**First impressions.** Austen's first title for the book was *First Impressions*. Both main characters judge too quickly.

**Pride and prejudice.** Darcy's pride and Elizabeth's prejudice both get in the way. Each has a bit of both.

**Marriage and money.** Women in 1811 often couldn't inherit property, so marriage decided a woman's future.

**Time to know someone.** Our version suggests that people often change their minds when they simply have more time together.

[Beat. Music sting.]

## Our what-if

[excited]

So what's the what-if? Here's the turning point we changed.

In the book, Jane falls ill at Netherfield and Elizabeth stays a few days to nurse her. In our story the rain doesn't stop, and they stay more than a week.

Everything after that moment is new, but the characters still act the way their authors wrote them.

[Beat.]

## Meet the author

[upbeat]

Before we go, here's a little about who wrote it.

Jane Austen was born in 1775, the daughter of a country vicar in Hampshire. She wrote the first version of this book in her early twenties and called it *First Impressions*. It was rejected, and she revised it for years. When it came out in 1813, her name wasn't on it. The title page said only "By the Author of *Sense and Sensibility*."

## The world of the book

[curious]

The book is set during the Regency, when wealthy families lived on income from land. Most women couldn't own much property or work in good jobs. A good marriage could decide a woman's whole future, and that's why Mrs Bennet worries so much.
