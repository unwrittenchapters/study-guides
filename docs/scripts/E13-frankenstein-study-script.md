## The book in brief

[Card slides in: 1818 · Geneva, the German forests and the Arctic, 1790s]

[upbeat, storytelling]

Victor Frankenstein, a young scientist, builds a creature from old parts and brings it to life. Horrified by how it looks, he runs away.

The creature, alone and rejected, hides beside the cottage of the De Lacey family. By listening through the wall he learns to speak and read, and he secretly cuts their firewood. When he finally meets the blind father, the son returns and drives him out.

Bitter and lonely, the creature takes revenge on Victor's family and asks Victor to make him a companion. Victor refuses, and the two chase each other to the Arctic, where the story is told to a ship's captain.

## The story, chapter by chapter

[storytelling, warm but lively]

Now for the story. Here's how our version unfolds, chapter by chapter.

[Chapter card flips in: THE HOVEL]

Chapter one. The Hovel.

The creature didn't have a name.

A young scientist called Victor Frankenstein had made him out of old parts and brought him to life with a spark. He was enormous, eight feet tall, and his face was strange and frightening to look at. The moment Victor saw him open his eyes, Victor ran away.

So the creature woke up in the world alone. He didn't know what he was, and he didn't know any words. He only knew that he was cold and hungry, and that people screamed whenever they saw him.

He went into the forest, where no one would see him. There he learned things slowly, the way a baby learns. He learned that berries were good to eat and a stream was good to drink from. He learned that the moon was beautiful, and so was birdsong. He found a fire some travellers had left. He learned it was warm, and that it would burn him if he touched it.

[Chapter card flips in: THE GOOD SPIRIT]

Chapter two. The Good Spirit.

More than anything, the creature wanted to help them.

At first he'd been stealing a little of their food at night. But when he saw how poor they were, he stopped, and he lived on nuts and roots from the forest instead. Then he had an idea.

Felix spent most of every day chopping wood for the fire. It was hard, slow work in the cold. So one night, when the family was asleep, the creature took Felix's axe and went out into the forest to cut wood. He was very strong, and in a few hours he'd cut enough for several days. He stacked it neatly by the cottage door and put the axe back. Then he crept into his hovel before the sun came up.

In the morning, Felix opened the door, stopped and stared.

[Chapter card flips in: THE KNOCK]

Chapter three. The Knock.

The creature had thought about it all winter.

He knew the young people would be frightened of his face. But the old man couldn't see. If he could talk to the old man first, alone, and make a friend of him, then the old man might speak for him to the others.

So he waited for a day when Felix, Agatha and Safie had gone for a long walk. His heart was beating so hard he thought it would break. He went round to the front of the cottage and knocked on the door.

The creature came in. He said he was a tired traveller and asked if he might rest by the fire for a while. Old De Lacey had been poor and lonely himself, and he said of course. He was sorry he had no food to offer, because his children were out.

They sat together by the fire, and slowly the creature began to tell his story.

[Chapter card flips in: THE LAST PAGE]

The Last Page.

It wasn't easy at first.

Safie wouldn't look at him for a week, and Felix watched him closely for a month. But the creature was patient and gentle, and he never stopped working. He dug the garden and mended the roof. He carried water and milked the cow, and he was very careful with the chickens. One evening, Safie was struggling with a hard French word, and he said it for her softly from the corner. She laughed out loud, and after that they were friends.

In the evenings after supper, old De Lacey played the guitar by the fire. The creature sat on the floor beside him, because no chair in the cottage was big enough. Sometimes the old man taught him to play. His fingers were much too large for the strings, but he tried. The family laughed with him, not at him, and he'd never known that feeling before.

## The big ideas

[curious]

**Responsibility.** Victor creates a life, then refuses to care for it.

**Loneliness.** The creature's cruelty grows out of rejection.

**Judging by appearance.** Only the blind man can hear who the creature really is.

**Learning.** The creature learns language, books and kindness by watching others.

## Our what-if

[excited]

So what's the what-if? Here's the turning point we changed.

In our story, the blind old man stands between his son and the creature and says, "This is our friend." Agatha realises who's been cutting their wood all winter.

Everything after that moment is new, but the characters still act the way their authors wrote them.

[Beat.]

## Meet the author

[upbeat]

Before we go, here's a little about who wrote it.

Mary Shelley was the daughter of two famous writers, Mary Wollstonecraft and William Godwin. In 1816, on a stormy summer by Lake Geneva, Lord Byron challenged his friends to write ghost stories. Eighteen-year-old Mary came up with *Frankenstein*. It was published in 1818, when she was twenty.

## The world of the book

[curious]

Scientists of Mary Shelley's time were experimenting with electricity, and some even tried to make dead frogs' legs twitch with it. People wondered whether science could create life. The book asks what responsibility a creator has for what they make.
