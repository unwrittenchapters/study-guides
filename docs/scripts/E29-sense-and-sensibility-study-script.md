## The book in brief

[Card slides in: 1811 · Sussex, Devonshire, London and Somerset]

[upbeat, storytelling]

When Mr Dashwood dies, his home, Norland Park in Sussex, goes to his son John. John promised his dying father he'd help his stepmother and half-sisters. But his wife, Fanny, talks him out of giving them almost anything. So Mrs Dashwood and her daughters Elinor, Marianne and Margaret move to a small cottage in Devonshire.

Calm, sensible Elinor loves Fanny's brother, Edward Ferrars, but learns he's been secretly engaged to Lucy Steele for four years. Passionate Marianne falls for the dashing John Willoughby, who drops her to marry a rich young woman. Each sister has to find her own way through heartbreak.

## The story, chapter by chapter

[storytelling, warm but lively]

Now for the story. Here's how it unfolds, chapter by chapter.

[Chapter card flips in: TWO SISTERS]

Chapter one. Two Sisters.

Elinor and Marianne Dashwood were sisters, but they couldn't have been more different.

Elinor kept her feelings quiet and tidy. Marianne let hers pour out like music. When their father died, they lost their home in Sussex and moved to a little cottage in Devonshire.

There, Marianne met John Willoughby. He was handsome and charming, and he loved the same poems she did. Then he suddenly went away. Months later in London, he sent her a cold letter, and soon he married a rich young lady.

Elinor had heartbreak of her own. The man she loved, Edward Ferrars, had been secretly engaged to Lucy Steele for four years. Elinor had promised Lucy to keep the secret, and she told no one, not even Marianne. When Marianne found out, she was ashamed of how little she'd noticed.

Now, on their way home, the sisters were staying at Cleveland, a big house in Somerset. On a hill above the lawn stood a little Grecian temple. Every evening, Marianne slipped out there alone.

[Chapter card flips in: THE GRECIAN TEMPLE]

Chapter two. The Grecian Temple.

But this time, Elinor saw her go.

It was a grey evening after rain, and the grass was long and wet. Elinor grabbed two shawls and hurried after her.

She found Marianne in the little temple at the top of the hill. Marianne was staring at the far blue hills. Somewhere beyond them, less than thirty miles away, was Willoughby's house.

"I hoped you'd come," Marianne said quietly.

Elinor wrapped a shawl round her sister's shoulders and sat down beside her. For a long time, neither of them spoke.

Then Marianne said, "How did you bear it, Elinor? All those months, and you never said a word."

"It wasn't easy," said Elinor. "But I didn't want to make you and Mother sad as well."

Marianne leaned her head on Elinor's shoulder and cried. This time, Elinor cried a little too.

[Chapter card flips in: DRY SHOES]

Chapter three. Dry Shoes.

They walked home before the dew fell, along the dry gravel path.

At the door, Elinor made Marianne change her wet shoes and stockings and sit by the fire.

After that, the sisters walked together every evening, and they kept to the paths. Marianne talked about Willoughby. Then, little by little, she talked about other things. She asked Elinor about Edward, and she really listened to the answers.

Colonel Brandon noticed the change. He was staying at Cleveland too, and he always quietly watched over Marianne. One evening, Marianne played the pianoforte in the drawing room. The others chattered, but Colonel Brandon simply listened, just as he had on the first night they'd met.

Marianne remembered that. When she finished, she turned round and thanked him for listening. Colonel Brandon was so surprised that he smiled.

[Chapter card flips in: THE LAST PAGE]

The Last Page.

They went home to Barton Cottage when the weather turned warm.

Marianne made a grand plan. She'd get up at six every morning and read for six hours a day. Elinor smiled, because it was still very like Marianne to do everything at full speed. Colonel Brandon rode over with books from his own library.

Then one afternoon, Edward Ferrars came to the cottage. Lucy Steele, he told them, had married his brother Robert instead. Edward was free.

Elinor hurried out of the room and burst into tears of joy. Marianne ran after her, laughing and crying at the same time. Edward asked Elinor to marry him, and she said yes.

That evening, the two sisters climbed the hill behind the cottage. Marianne didn't race ahead this time. She walked arm in arm with her sister, all the way home.

## The big ideas

[curious]

**Sense and sensibility.** Elinor's good sense and Marianne's strong feelings both matter, and each sister learns from the other.

**Money and marriage.** Women like the Dashwoods had little money and few ways to earn it, so marriage often decided their future.

**Secrets and honesty.** Lucy's secret engagement causes much of the pain.

**Sisters.** Elinor and Marianne's love for each other holds the whole story together.

## The real ending

[clearly, storytelling]

So what actually happens in the book?

In the book, Marianne takes long walks in the wet grass at Cleveland and falls dangerously ill. Elinor nurses her, Colonel Brandon fetches their mother, and Marianne recovers and decides to live more wisely. Lucy marries Edward's brother Robert, so Edward marries Elinor, and Marianne later marries Colonel Brandon.

[thoughtfully]

Why did Jane Austen choose it? Austen shows that strong feelings need good sense to guide them, and that the sisters find happiness by looking after each other.

[Beat.]

## Meet the author

[upbeat]

Before we go, here's a little about who wrote it.

Jane Austen was born in 1775 at Steventon in Hampshire, where her father was the rector. She wrote a first version of this story, called *Elinor and Marianne*, when she was about nineteen. She revised it at Chawton, and it came out in 1811, signed only "By a Lady." It was her first published novel, and it earned her a hundred and forty pounds.

## The world of the book

[curious]

In Austen's England, a family's land and money usually passed to sons, not daughters. Young women like the Dashwoods often had only small sums of their own, so they depended on marrying well. Good manners mattered too, and people were expected to hide strong feelings in public.
