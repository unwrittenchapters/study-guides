## The book in brief

[Card slides in: 1851 · Nantucket and the Atlantic, Indian and Pacific Oceans, 1840s]

[upbeat, storytelling]

A young sailor who asks us to call him Ishmael signs on to a whaling ship from Nantucket called the *Pequod*. Its captain, Ahab, has lost a leg to a giant white sperm whale called Moby Dick, and he's sworn to find it. He nails a gold coin to the mast for whoever sees the whale first.

The book follows the long voyage across the oceans, mixing adventure with chapters about whales, ships and the sea. Near the end, in a chapter called "The Symphony," Ahab talks gently with his first mate, Starbuck, about the wife and child he left at home. Starbuck begs him to turn back.

## The story, chapter by chapter

[storytelling, warm but lively]

Now for the story. Here's how it unfolds, chapter by chapter.

[Chapter card flips in: LOOMINGS]

Chapter one. Loomings.

Ishmael goes to sea whenever he feels gloomy. One cold December he signs on to the *Pequod*, a whaling ship from Nantucket. Her captain, Ahab, has a leg made of whalebone. He lost the real one to a huge white whale called Moby Dick.

Ahab nails a gold coin to the mast for whoever sights the whale first. The crew cheers. Only Starbuck, the careful first mate, stays quiet and thinks of his wife and son.

Starbuck came to sea to hunt whales for their oil, as an honest living. He didn't come to chase one whale across the world for revenge. He looks at the coin for a long time, then goes below to the picture of his family.

[Chapter card flips in: THE MAST-HEAD]

Chapter two. The Mast-Head.

The *Pequod* sails for months, around Africa and into the Pacific. Ishmael takes his turn at the masthead and drifts into daydreams. On calm golden days the sea is like glass.

Starbuck thinks about the hill above Nantucket harbour, where his wife has promised to carry their little boy every morning to watch for his father's sail. Ahab paces the deck and looks at the coin.

Ishmael admits he's a poor lookout. Up at the top of the mast, a hundred feet above the deck, he feels the whole ocean is a dream, and he's part of it. Melville writes about those calm days in chapters called "The Mast-Head" and "The Gilder."

[Chapter card flips in: THE SYMPHONY]

Chapter three. The Symphony.

On a clear steel-blue morning, Ahab leans over the rail and drops a tear into the sea. He tells Starbuck about forty years at sea, and the wife and little boy he left behind. Starbuck begs him to turn for home. He tells Ahab about the hill, and the boy watching for a sail, and Ahab says he's seen mild blue days like this in Nantucket too.

In the book, Ahab looks away. Tonight he doesn't. He calls the crew, the yards swing round, and the *Pequod* turns her bow toward home. Up at the masthead, Ishmael watches the long white wake curve away behind them.

[Chapter card flips in: THE LAST PAGE]

The Last Page.

Ahab gives the gold coin to Starbuck, for the boy. The ship sails the long way home, and the white whale swims on, free. One summer morning, a little boy on the hill above Nantucket sees a sail. Years later, Ishmael writes the whole story down, and begins it with the same three words.

## The big ideas

[curious]

**Obsession.** Ahab can't let go of his anger at the whale. It pushes everything else out of his life.

**Home.** Starbuck keeps thinking of his wife and son. The calm chapter "The Symphony" shows Ahab remembering home too.

**Choice.** In the book, Ahab says he can't stop. A what-if asks what happens if he chooses differently.

**The size of the sea.** Melville fills the book with facts about whales and oceans to show how huge and mysterious the natural world is.

## Our what-if

[curious]

In an alternative scenario, what if on that calm blue morning, Ahab listens when Starbuck begs him to turn for home?

[thoughtful]

So what could have happened?

[Path card: A · The hunt goes on]

Path A. Ahab refuses, as he does in the book, and the hunt goes on. It could happen: Ahab has sworn the whole crew to the hunt. But this is the one moment he's thinking of home.

[Path card: B · Starbuck takes over]

Path B. Starbuck takes the ship from Ahab by force. It could happen: Starbuck once aims a musket at Ahab. But he lowers it. He believes in duty.

[Path card: C · Ahab turns for home]

Path C. Ahab thinks of his wife and young son, and orders the ship about. This one fits the characters: Melville shows Ahab crying as he remembers home. Starbuck asks at the moment Ahab is softest.

## The real ending

[clearly, storytelling]

So what actually happens in the book?

In the book, Ahab looks away, and the chase goes on. Over three days, Moby Dick wrecks the boats and sinks the Pequod. Ahab is dragged under by his own harpoon line, and only Ishmael survives, floating on Queequeg's coffin.

[thoughtfully]

Why did Herman Melville choose it? Melville shows where obsession leads. Ahab had every chance to turn back.

[Beat.]

## Meet the author

[upbeat]

Before we go, here's a little about who wrote it.

Herman Melville was born in New York in 1819. As a young man he sailed on a whaling ship to the Pacific and later jumped ship in the Marquesas Islands. He wrote *Moby-Dick* at his farmhouse, Arrowhead, in Massachusetts. The book sold poorly in his lifetime, and he died almost forgotten.

## The world of the book

[curious]

In the 1840s and 1850s, whaling was a huge industry. Whale oil lit lamps across America and Europe, and ships from Nantucket and New Bedford stayed at sea for three or four years at a time, sailing all the way around the world.
