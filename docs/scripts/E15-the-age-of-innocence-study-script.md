## The book in brief

[Card slides in: 1920 · New York in the 1870s, and Paris about thirty years later]

[upbeat, storytelling]

Newland Archer, a young lawyer from old New York society, is engaged to the lovely, proper May Welland. Then he meets May's cousin, Countess Ellen Olenska, who has left her cruel husband in Europe and come home.

Ellen is honest and unconventional, and Newland falls in love with her. He asks her to run away with him, but she refuses, because it would hurt May. Newland marries May, and Ellen returns to Paris.

Decades pass. After May's death, Newland's son Dallas takes him to Paris and tells him May always knew he'd given up the thing he wanted most. Outside Ellen's apartment, Newland sits on a bench, decides not to go up, and walks away.

## The story, chapter by chapter

[storytelling, warm but lively]

Now for the story. Our sleep story for this book is called "Newland Goes Up." Here's how it unfolds, chapter by chapter.

[Chapter card flips in: THE OPERA]

Chapter one. The Opera.

Wharton begins her book on a winter night at the opera.

So it's about 1870, and a famous singer is on stage. Everybody who matters in New York is in the audience.

Newland Archer was there too. He was a young lawyer from one of the old families, handsome and clever and a little bit bored. He arrived late on purpose, because arriving late was the fashionable thing to do. Through his opera glasses, he looked across the theatre at the Mingott family's box.

May Welland sat in it, young and fair and lovely in a white dress, with a bunch of lilies-of-the-valley in her lap. Newland was about to become engaged to her, and every day he sent her a fresh box of lilies-of-the-valley. She was everything a young man in his world was supposed to want.

That evening, someone new sat beside her.

[Chapter card flips in: THE YELLOW ROSES]

Chapter two. The Yellow Roses.

The next morning, Newland went to the florist as he always did, to send May her lilies-of-the-valley.

There in the shop, he saw a bunch of yellow roses. They were a deep, warm, sunny yellow, the colour of a summer afternoon, and nothing else in the cold grey city looked like them.

He sent them to Ellen without a card.

It was the beginning of something neither of them could stop.

For a whole winter, they saw each other when they could, at parties and at the opera. Once they were both at a great country house up the Hudson River, in the snow. They walked alone together to a little old house by the frozen water and sat by a fire. They said almost nothing, and they understood each other completely.

At last, Newland asked her to let him break his engagement. They could go away together, to Europe or anywhere.

And Ellen said no.

[Chapter card flips in: PARIS]

Chapter three. Paris.

When Newland Archer was fifty-seven, his eldest son took him to Paris.

Dallas was young and cheerful and modern, the way young men were in those days. He was about to be married and had a business trip to make to France, and he talked his father into coming with him. One afternoon at their hotel, Dallas said quite casually that they'd been invited to call on Madame Olenska. They were expected at half past five that very day.

Newland hadn't seen her for nearly thirty years.

They walked there together through the golden afternoon. They crossed the river and went through the wide, beautiful streets towards the great gold dome of Les Invalides. As they walked, Dallas told his father something.

[Chapter card flips in: THE LAST PAGE]

The Last Page.

Her drawing room was full of books, pictures, soft lamps and flowers. It wasn't like any room in New York. It was very like a little house in an unfashionable street, long ago.

Dallas had been talking to her about the weather in Paris, his wedding and his plans. When his father came in, he looked from one of them to the other, and for once in his life he was quite silent. Then he remembered an urgent appointment somewhere else. He kissed Madame Olenska's hand and left them alone.

They sat in two chairs by the open window, with the evening and the sounds of the square below coming in.

For a long time they said very little. They'd never needed to say much. She asked about his children. He told her about May, and what Dallas had told him on the walk. She listened with wet eyes, and she said May had been braver than any of them.

[curious]

## Meet the author

[upbeat]

Before we go, here's a little about who wrote it.

Edith Wharton was born in 1862 into the same rich old New York world she describes. She was expected to marry and run a home, not to write. She wrote anyway, and became one of America's great novelists. In 1921 she became the first woman to win the Pulitzer Prize for a novel.

## The world of the book

[curious]

Wharton wrote the book in 1920, just after the First World War, looking back fifty years to the New York of her childhood. That world of strict rules, opera boxes and careful manners had almost vanished. The title is a little ironic, because the people in it aren't really innocent.
