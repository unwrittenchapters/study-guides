## The book in brief

[Card slides in: 1872 · London and around the world, October to December 1872]

[upbeat, storytelling]

Phileas Fogg is an exact, wealthy London gentleman. At the Reform Club he bets twenty thousand pounds that he can travel round the world in eighty days. He leaves that night with his new French servant, Passepartout.

A detective named Fix, who thinks Fogg robbed the Bank of England, follows them. In India, Fogg rescues a young woman named Aouda. They cross the world by steamship, train, elephant and sledge, and when the coal runs out on the Atlantic, Fogg burns his ship's wooden parts.

Fix arrests Fogg in Liverpool, and he reaches London five minutes late. Ruined, he and Aouda agree to marry. Then Passepartout discovers they gained a day by travelling east, and Fogg races to the club and wins.

## The story, chapter by chapter

[storytelling, warm but lively]

Now for the story. Our sleep story for this book is called "The Eighty-First Day." Here's how it unfolds, chapter by chapter.

[Chapter card flips in: THE WAGER]

Chapter one. The Wager.

There was no man in London more exact than Mr Phileas Fogg.

He lived alone in a quiet house at Number 7, Savile Row. He got up at the same minute every morning, and he walked to his club, the Reform Club, in the same number of steps. He ate breakfast, lunch and dinner there, at the same table and the same hours. In the evening he played cards, and he went home at exactly midnight.

Nobody knew where his money came from, or whether he'd ever travelled. He was polite and calm and rich, and as regular as a clock. That was all anyone knew about him.

On the morning our story begins, Mr Fogg had just dismissed his servant. The poor man had brought him shaving water that was two degrees too cool.

[Chapter card flips in: THE LONG WAY ROUND]

Chapter two. The Long Way Round.

They went east.

They crossed the Channel to France and took the train down through France and Italy to the port of Brindisi. Then a steamship carried them across the Mediterranean and through the Suez Canal, the long, narrow waterway cut through the desert. They sailed down the Red Sea, past Arabia, and across the Indian Ocean to Bombay.

Mr Fogg saw almost none of it. He sat in his cabin playing cards and wrote the time of every arrival in a little notebook. Passepartout saw all of it and loved every minute.

They weren't travelling alone, though they didn't know it. A detective called Fix had decided Mr Fogg must be the bank robber, running away with the money. So he followed them all the way round the world, waiting for a chance to arrest him.

[Chapter card flips in: SATURDAY]

Chapter three. Saturday.

They landed at Liverpool just before noon on what they believed was the very last day. They had nine hours left, and London was only six hours away by train.

And at that moment, Detective Fix finally arrested Mr Fogg.

It was a mistake, of course. The real bank robber had been caught three days before. But by the time anyone found out and let Mr Fogg go, it was too late. He took a special train to London as fast as it would go, and he reached the station at ten minutes to nine.

He was five minutes late.

Mr Fogg went home to Savile Row very quietly with Aouda and Passepartout. He'd spent almost all his money on the trip, and now he'd lost the bet as well. He was ruined.

He didn't complain, or even sigh. He only went upstairs to his room and shut the door.

[Chapter card flips in: THE LAST PAGE]

The Last Page.

The gentlemen of the Reform Club were rather embarrassed to collect their money.

The next week, all five of them came round to Savile Row to ask what on earth had happened. Mr Fogg told them, quite calmly. They went back to their club and talked about it for years. Phileas Fogg had gone round the world in seventy-nine days, they said, and then sat at home and missed the finish on purpose.

Nobody in London understood it. But then, nobody in London had met Aouda.

Mr Fogg and Aouda were married on the Monday, as planned. They sold the house in Savile Row and moved to a smaller one with a garden, in a quiet square. Mr Fogg had very little money left, but to his surprise, he didn't mind. He took a job keeping accounts at a shipping office, where his love of exact numbers made him very useful.

## The big ideas

[curious]

**Time.** Fogg measures every minute, until he decides some things matter more.

**Technology.** Verne celebrates the new railways and steamships that shrank the world.

**Character beneath the surface.** Aouda sees Fogg's kindness under his calm.

**What's worth winning.** Verne asks whether the trip was worth it. Our story answers differently.

## Our what-if

[excited]

So what's the what-if? Here's the turning point we changed.

In our story, Fogg learns he has forty minutes left, then closes his watch, turns it to the wall, and asks for tea.

Everything after that moment is new, but the characters still act the way their authors wrote them.

[Beat.]

## Meet the author

[upbeat]

Before we go, here's a little about who wrote it.

Jules Verne was born in Nantes, a port city in France, in 1828. As a boy he dreamed of going to sea. He trained as a lawyer but loved writing, and his publisher, Pierre-Jules Hetzel, helped him create a series of "extraordinary voyages." Verne researched timetables, maps and science to make his journeys believable.

## The world of the book

[curious]

In 1869 the Suez Canal opened and a railway crossed the United States for the first time. A railway across India had also opened up. Suddenly a trip around the world in eighty days really was possible. In 1889, the reporter Nellie Bly tried to beat Fogg's record, and did it in seventy-two days.
